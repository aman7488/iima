# Painter Brand Preference Insights

*Interview with a painting contractor, conducted for the Marketing II group project on Asian Paints (Group C12). The interviewee is not named. Translated from Hindi and English into English; timestamps are from the original recording.*

## Summary

### The painter's role and brand choice

- The painter has 15 years of experience, mostly whole-house painting: emulsion, distemper, texture and waterproofing.
- Customers often just say "Asian Paints"; the painter decides the variant, number of coats, putty and primer.
    - In about 70 out of 100 homes, the final decision follows the painter's advice.
- Why Asian Paints leads: available in every shop, instant shades from the tinting machine, trust, and easy points and returns.
- Other brands: Berger, Nerolac, and a new brand (heard as "Birla Opus") that is offering painters large schemes.

### Selection factors, price and coverage

- Priorities: finish and coverage; stock at the shop and consistent shades; schemes (points, cashback, scholarships, insurance); and the relationship with the dealer.
- Labour is charged per square foot, so the real benefit of good coverage is time saved: one extra house a month.
- If the price gap is ₹100–200 per can, the painter steers the customer to the better brand; if the gap is large, the customer buys the cheaper one.
- Customers barely understand premium versus economy paints; the painter applies samples on the wall to show the difference.

### Product problems and brand comparison

- Problems: paint blistering from damp, colour differences between two cans of the same shade, strong smell of cheap paint, and putty that dries slowly.
- Little difference in premium ranges; the real difference shows in economy ranges.
    - Asian Paints: smooth finish, consistent shade. Berger WeatherCoat: very good for exterior walls.
    - Nerolac: some products feel thin and sometimes need an extra coat; its low-odour paint is good.

### Dealers, training and expectations from companies

- Two or three regular shops, one a 10-year relationship: credit, site delivery and returns of unused cans.
- The dealer praises the brand that gives him the best margin, but the final decision is the painter's, because the work goes out under his name.
- Has attended Asian Paints and Berger training sessions and learned machine painting, but most painters attend for the schemes and gifts.
- Suggestions: faster payment of points, small loans during the monsoon, training and certificates in his own city, customer leads through the company's app or website, and above all the same shade every time.

**[00:20] Painter:** Madam, it's been 15 years in this line. I came from the village and started as a helper under a contractor, then after 5–6 years I started my own work. I mostly do houses: painting whole flats or houses, emulsion, distemper, and sometimes texture and waterproofing too.

But which product, say Royale or Tractor Emulsion, they don't know, so they leave it to us. I ask their budget, look at the condition of the wall and advise them, and if the budget is tight I suggest a cheaper brand like Berger or Indigo.

**[01:39] Painter:** The quality stays almost the same; in about 70 out of 100 homes, the final decision is made on my advice.

**[02:01] Interviewer:** Okay. So in your view, painters have a big say in which paint, or which variant, is finally used, right?

**[02:11] Painter:** Yes madam, absolutely. A painter's word carries a lot of weight. The customer thinks, he does this every day, so he'll know which paint will last.

**[02:23] Painter:** The customer may name the brand, but which variant, how many coats, which putty and primer, the painter almost always decides that. And the shopkeepers know it too.

**[02:53] Interviewer:** And why Asian Paints? Is no other brand popular, or what are the reasons everyone goes for Asian Paints?

**[03:01] Painter:** Madam, Asian Paints has the biggest name, because for years it has been available everywhere, in every small and big shop. And the colour machine makes any shade instantly. The customer trusts that if it's expensive, it must be good.

**[03:16] Painter:** And their scheme is good for us painters too; points come quickly, and returning or exchanging stock is easy. Other brands are popular as well, like Berger, Nerolac, and now Birla Opus has come in, which is offering painters big schemes.

**[03:33] Painter:** But in the customer's mind, the first name that comes is still Asian Paints.

**[03:39] Interviewer:** Understood. Now tell me from your own perspective, what influences you to choose one paint over any other brand?

**[04:02] Painter:** Second, the stock should always be available at the shop and the shade should come out the same. Third, to be honest, I also look at the scheme: points, cashback, and sometimes a scholarship for the children's education or insurance.

**[04:16] Painter:** And finally, the relationship with the shopkeeper, who gives credit and delivers stock to the site on time.

**[04:26] Interviewer:** Hmm. So is coverage an important consideration? For example, if two brands offer similar quality but one needs less paint to cover the same area, would that influence your recommendation?

**[04:41] Painter:** Yes madam, it definitely makes a difference. If less paint is needed, the customer saves money, and I can give a cheaper quotation, which improves my chances of getting the job. But one thing: we mostly charge labour per square foot, so coverage doesn't directly increase my earnings.

**[05:00] Painter:** But if the paint spreads quickly and the job is done in fewer coats, I save time, and I can take on one more house a month. That's the real benefit for me.

**[05:18] Interviewer:** Okay. And is there any particular problem you face while working with paint products?

**[05:25] Painter:** Yes madam, several. The biggest problem is damp. After the rains there is moisture in the wall, the paint blisters or peels, and the customer blames the painter.

**[05:38] Painter:** Second, sometimes two cans of the same shade come out slightly different in colour, so patches show on the wall. Third, cheap paint has a very strong smell; if there are children or elderly people at home, they complain.

**[05:52] Painter:** And finally, putty takes time to dry, so the job drags on and the customer gets impatient.

**[06:05] Interviewer:** Okay. And do you see any major difference between Asian Paints, Berger and Nerolac in terms of product performance?

**[06:14] Painter:** Madam, honestly, there isn't a big difference in the top-end products. The premium emulsions of all three work well. Asian Paints' finish comes out a little smoother, and the shade is the same every time, so there's more trust.

**[06:31] Painter:** Berger's WeatherCoat is very good for exterior walls, and the price is a little lower. Some of Nerolac's stock feels thin; sometimes you need an extra coat for coverage.

**[06:44] Painter:** But their low-odour paint is fine. The real difference mostly shows in the economy range; there, quality varies a lot from company to company.

**[07:25] Painter:** What's the next question?

**[07:39] Interviewer:** Now I want to know how important price is when you recommend a particular paint brand.

**[07:45] Painter:** Price is very important, especially in a town like ours. Most customers say upfront, "The whole house should be done within this amount." Then I choose the best product within that budget.

**[07:57] Painter:** If the difference is ₹100–200 per can, I push them towards the better brand, so there are no complaints later. But if the difference is big, the customer takes the cheaper one, whatever I say.

**[09:39] Interviewer:** And does the dealer influence which brand you buy?
